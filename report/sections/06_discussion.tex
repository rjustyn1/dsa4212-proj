\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the results mean}

In our setting, adding SOAP's rescaling step does not make Moonlight Muon a
better optimizer. It speeds up the start of training, but the advantage is
temporary, small even at its peak (about 1.5M tokens), and almost entirely
reversed by the end. The 40M-token tuning runs point the same way: they use a
complete but short learning-rate schedule, and in them SOAP--Muon finished with
a lower loss than the baseline by 0.060 at $1.2\times10^{-3}$ and by 0.032 at
$4.8\times10^{-3}$. The rescaling step therefore seems to help early in training
or in short runs, but not over a longer run. One possible explanation, which
our experiments do not test, is that giving each direction its own step size
helps most early on, when gradients change quickly and differ strongly between
directions, and adds little once training settles. The results also show why
comparisons made partway through training can mislead: judged at 40M tokens,
SOAP--Muon looks clearly better, but at 200M tokens it is slightly worse, in
line with the observation that optimizer rankings can change during training
\cite{wen2025fantastic}.

The results also show how much the comparison depends on tuning. At the
first-round rate of $1.2\times10^{-3}$, SOAP--Muon looked better for all five
seeds. Raising the learning rate to its properly tuned value improved the
baseline's final loss by 0.027, about four times SOAP--Muon's largest average
effect on the final loss in either experiment, and removed most of
SOAP--Muon's advantage. Although the two experiments used different seeds, this
suggests that much of the earlier advantage simply made up for an under-tuned
baseline, a pattern that Wen et~al.\ report across many optimizers
\cite{wen2025fantastic}. In practice, tuning the learning rate of the existing
optimizer brought a far larger gain than adding the SOAP step.

Finally, the variation between repeated runs is about as large as the effect we
are measuring. Repeating one run with the same seed, data, and settings on a
different GPU model changed its final loss by 0.0046, about 70\% of
SOAP--Muon's average effect on the final loss, and its curve area by 0.0047,
more than three times the average effect on the curve area. Small numerical
differences, for example between GPU models, can grow over thousands of
training steps, so identical settings do not guarantee identical results.
Pairing runs by seed therefore removes less noise than we had assumed, and five
seeds cannot detect effects much smaller than a few thousandths of the loss.

\subsection{Limitations}

We studied one small model (33.7M parameters), one fixed order of training
data, and one GPU type, so the results may differ for larger models, other
architectures, other data orders, or training on many GPUs. The seeds vary only
the initial weights. We tuned the learning rate on one seed, in steps of a
factor of two, and on 40M-token runs; the best rate for 200M-token runs may be
different, and may differ between the two optimizers. We tested only one
placement of the SOAP step (both MLP matrices) and one interval for updating its
rotation. Measuring the loss every 10M tokens limits how precisely we can tell
when each loss level was reached. Finally, we did not measure what happens
inside the optimizer, for example how the learned rotation changes during
training, so we cannot explain why the early advantage fades. A study that
tracks these quantities and tunes the learning rate for the full training
length is a natural next step.
